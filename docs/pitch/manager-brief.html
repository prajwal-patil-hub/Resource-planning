Where the week goes

We deliver client change requests and bug fixes with no record of who is doing what. This is a proposal to fix that — without timesheets, without estimates, and without anything for you to configure.

Four things that happen to us

These aren't hypothetical. Each one has happened, and each one is a direct consequence of work being assigned verbally and recorded nowhere.

Work is agreed verbally, then forgotten. We find out we committed to something when the client chases us for it.
A task sits untouched for two days because the person was busy elsewhere. Nobody notices until it's already late.
A fix takes fifteen minutes, and we learn that the next day. We have no basis for telling a client how long anything takes.
The person handling a client's work goes on leave. The client keeps waiting, believing it's in progress.

Underneath all four is one mechanism

Urgent work pushes planned work aside, and nothing records that it happened.

A client raises a P0 bug. A developer sets aside the change request they were working on. Nobody writes this down. Two weeks later that change request is described as “late” — when in fact it was displaced, very often by the same client's own urgent request.

We currently have no way to show that. So we absorb the blame for it.

What changes

Today

  • Work lives in email, memory and conversation
  • Nobody can say what the team is working on right now
  • Delays are discovered by the client first
  • “Why is this late?” is a matter of opinion
  • Overload is noticed when someone misses something

With this

  • Every request has a record and an owner
  • One screen shows who has what, right now
  • Work that stops moving surfaces on its own
  • Lateness has a recorded cause, with evidence
  • Overload is visible before it costs us a deadline

What it asks of the team

Almost nothing, and this is deliberate. Anything that takes longer than telling a colleague simply won't get used.

WhoWhat they do
Developers & QA Type a one-line title when work reaches them. Tap a status as it moves. That's the whole commitment — no timesheets, no estimates.
Team leads Use one screen instead of asking around. Assign from it.
You Nothing to set up, nothing to administer, nobody to chase. Look at it when you want to know where we stand.

Everything else — how loaded each person is, what's at risk, how long things actually take, where our time goes by client — is calculated from those two actions. Nobody is asked to report on themselves.

What it won't do

  • It won't tell you someone has “4 hours free on Tuesday.” That requires time tracking we're not going to do, and any tool claiming it without that data is guessing at you confidently.
  • It won't give a single guaranteed delivery date. It gives a realistic range based on how long similar work has actually taken us — for example, “four out of five bugs like this finished within three days.”
  • It's not useful on day one for forecasting. It needs three or four weeks of real use before it knows our patterns. Knowing who is working on what, and what has stalled, works immediately.

What I'm asking for

  1. Thirty minutes of your time — I want to hear what you actually decide each week and what you wish you knew, before I build the wrong thing.
  2. Agreement to try it with one team for a month.
  3. Your backing that recording work is expected — not enforced, just expected. That alone decides whether this works.

If after a month the team isn't recording at least four out of five requests, I'll say so plainly and we stop. That number is the honest test of whether this is worth continuing.

Built in-house. No licence cost, no external service, no client data leaving our systems. Full requirements and design decisions are documented and available for review.
